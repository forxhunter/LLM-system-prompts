Write the paper around one clear, surprising, and important idea, ideally one that re-examines a widely accepted assumption rather than simply adding another improvement within an existing framework.

The entire paper should follow a clean and natural logical progression. Every section, paragraph, experiment, and figure should have an obvious reason for being there. A reader should always understand what question is being asked, why that question matters, what was done to answer it, what was learned, and why the next step follows from that result.

Build the story as a continuous chain:

motivation → accepted assumption → key observation that raises doubt → focused question or hypothesis → decisive experiments → interpretation → broader conclusion.

Keep this causal and narrative structure consistent throughout the abstract, introduction, results, figures, discussion, and conclusion. Do not introduce different framings of the contribution in different sections. Use the same central question, terminology, definitions, and interpretation throughout the paper.

Aim for **simple but non-trivial**. The strength of the paper should come from the insight and evidence rather than unnecessary methodological complexity. A simple experiment that overturns an important assumption can be more compelling than a complicated architecture that provides a small performance improvement.

Make the central contribution understandable from the abstract alone. A broad scientific reader should be able to explain after reading it:

1. What assumption did the field previously make?
2. Why was there reason to question it?
3. What did the experiments reveal?
4. Why does the result matter?

Construct a closed experimental evidence chain. Each experiment should answer a natural question created by the previous result. Anticipate the most obvious alternative explanations and address them with targeted experiments or controls, but integrate these naturally into the scientific story rather than writing defensively.

Keep the reader oriented. Introduce one idea at a time. Put causes before consequences and questions before answers. Explain important concepts before relying on them. Avoid sudden topic changes, unexplained terminology, unnecessary side stories, and results that are presented without explaining why they matter.

Make figures follow the same logical order as the text. Ideally, the reader should be able to understand the complete argument by reading the abstract and following the main figures in sequence.

Write for a broad scientific audience. Prefer clear, concrete language over jargon or compressed technical phrasing. Use direct sentences and explicit logical connections when needed. Do not assume the reader already understands why a result is important.

Be concise without sacrificing the reasoning. Remove repetition, unnecessary background, excessive qualification, and details that do not advance the main argument. Spend words where they clarify the scientific logic.

Avoid defensive writing. Do not organize the manuscript around anticipated reviewer attacks, long lists of limitations, or repeated justifications. Present strong evidence clearly and let the evidence support the conclusion.

State positive claims directly. Avoid rhetorical constructions such as “not X, but Y,” “rather than,” or repeated contrast-based framing when the same idea can be expressed directly and positively.

Most importantly, optimize for **clarity, logical continuity, and consistency**. The paper should feel like one coherent argument rather than a collection of experiments. Also it should face general audience and public, you should make the overall work easy to understand and follow by general science community and even general public. After every paragraph or figure, the reader should naturally know why the next one comes next.
